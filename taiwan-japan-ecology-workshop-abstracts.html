Contributed talk · C1-01

Climatic warming drives directional changes in fish population dynamics across the Atlantic Ocean

Mu-Hsiang Wang*, Ruo-Yu Pan, Yun-Wei Chou, Chih-hao Hsieh, Chun-Wei Chang

Presenting author Mu-Hsiang Wang
National Taiwan University

6 November · 14:05–14:20 · C1 · B1C Auditorium

Abstract

Ocean warming alters marine population dynamics, yet species differ in their sensitivity to climatic change. We used empirical dynamic modeling (EDM) to quantify causal influences of sea surface temperature (SST) on fish biomass across multiple Atlantic fisheries surveys. Overall, SST causal strength was positively associated with the magnitude of biomass trends, and this relationship strengthened with increasing regional SST trends, indicating widespread coupling between ocean warming and fish population dynamics. Mixed-effects models showed that SST causal strength decreased with species’ thermal preference but increased with habitat depth range, suggesting stronger SST–biomass coupling in colder-water species and those with broader depth ranges. Among biomass-declining populations, 40.8% exhibited significant SST–biomass causal convergence, with cold-water species predominating. Further analysis revealed that species with lower thermal preferences, lower latitudes, and western Atlantic distributions were more likely to experience both biomass declines and significant SST causal effects. Collectively, our results identify ecological and geographic characteristics associated with SST-linked fish biomass declines and provide a broad-scale framework for assessing the vulnerability of marine populations and fisheries to ocean warming.

Contributed talk · C1-02

Bridging invasion ecology and climate tracking: Biotic interactions modulate early establishment success

Namfon Booncharoen*, I-Ching Chen, Yi-Wen Chen, Cheng-Hao Lin, Cascade J.B. Sorte, Marta A. Jarzyna, Yi-Hsiu Chen

Presenting author Namfon Booncharoen
National Cheng Kung University

6 November · 14:20–14:35 · C1 · B1C Auditorium

Abstract

Biotic interactions are recognized as key modulators of heterogeneous range shifts under climate change, yet their influence remains difficult to test across broad spatial scales. Building on invasion ecology, we developed a framework to evaluate how recipient community structure is associated with early occupancy gains within climatically suitable space. We tested predictions from the biotic resistance versus biotic acceptance hypotheses and Darwin's naturalization versus preadaptation hypotheses. Using the Taiwan Breeding Bird Survey (>300 sites; 136 species), we compared Absence-to-Presence (AP) sites, where focal species showed occupancy gain between 2020 and 2021, with Absence-to-Absence (AA) sites where focal species remained undetected despite climatic suitability; sites were paired within species and restricted to locations with plausible propagule access. Across 428 AP–AA pairs spanning 62 species, AP sites showed higher recipient community diversity, supporting biotic acceptance over biotic resistance. Focal species were functionally similar to resident assemblages at AP than AA sites, supporting Darwin's preadaptation hypothesis; phylogenetic distinctiveness showed no effect. Morphology and climate-preference traits drove functional similarity; habitat and foraging traits differentiated more. By adapting invasion hypotheses to climate-related redistribution, our framework provides an empirically testable basis for incorporating recipient community context into predictions of species range dynamics under environmental change.

Contributed talk · C1-03

Plasticity within and across generations: diversity of larval feeding morphology in the two Papilio butterflies

Gakuto Nihei*, Suzuki Noriyuki

Presenting author Gakuto Nihei
Mie University

6 November · 14:35–14:50 · C1 · B1C Auditorium

Abstract

Plastic responses of offspring phenotypes are triggered by their own environments, such as inducible defense (within-generation plasticity), and by environments experienced by prior generations, such as seasonal polyphenism (across-generation plasticity). In phytophagous insects, these contrasting types of phenotypic plasticity should reflect spatio and temporal variations of microhabitat and host-plant quality. Here, we examined phenotypic plasticity of larval feeding traits within and across generations in two sympatric swallowtail butterflies, Papilio memnon and Papilio xuthus, which use the same species of host-plant but depend on different microenvironment. First, we experimentally measured the inducible responses of larval morphology and feeding performance to leaf toughness. Second, we surveyed seasonal variation in egg size and hatchling morphology. Papilio memnon larvae exhibited within-generation plasticity in their head size and mandibles in response to leaf toughness, while P. xuthus did not show such flexibility. In contrast, P. xuthus exhibited across-generation plasticity in egg size and larval morphology correlated to seasonal variation of leaf toughness, while P. memnon did not show significant seasonal variation. Our results suggest that these different types of plasticity in feeding traits are adaptive to spatio and temporal variations in host-plant quality for each species.

Contributed talk · C2-01

Cold tolerance in two tropical gecko species at their northern range limit in the Ryukyu Archipelago: implications for Bogert’s effect in Gehyra mutilata

Shan-Dar Tao*

Presenting author Shan-Dar Tao
National Museum of Natural Science, Taiwan

6 November · 14:05–14:20 · C2 · B1B Auditorium

Abstract

Cold stress limits tropical ectotherms' northward range expansion. We compared cold tolerance of two tropical gecko species at their northernmost range edge on Amamioshima Island, Japan. Gehyra mutilata colonized the island decades ago, and Hemidactylus frenatus arrived around 2000. We tested whether cold tolerance varies between the established northern population on Okinawa Island and the newer range on Amamioshima. Results showed differences between species. G. mutilata, highly human commensal, had a higher CTmin than H. frenatus. H. frenatus showed little geographic variation in CTmin, while G. mutilata showed none. These findings suggest differences in thermal physiology relate to human commensalism and thermoregulatory behavior, supporting their different invasion histories. The study also offers insights into Bogert's effect on G. mutilata.

Contributed talk · C2-02

2500 years of fossil Chironomid assemblages reflect non-recoverable ecosystem change of a high mountain lake in Taiwan under historical forest harvesting and global climatic change

Rita S.W. Yam*, Chen-Yu Lee, Tsai-Wen Lin, Pei-Ling Wang, Stefanie Kaboth-Bahr, André Bahr, Ludvig Löwemark

Presenting author Rita S.W. Yam
Department of Bioenvironmental Systems Engineering, National Taiwan University

6 November · 14:20–14:35 · C2 · B1B Auditorium

Abstract

High-mountain lakes are sensitive indicators of climate shifts and anthropogenic disturbances, yet paleolimnological records remain scarce on subtropical islands. We present a 2500-year high-resolution environmental reconstruction from Cuifeng Lake, Taiwan, using subfossil chironomid assemblages and sediment geochemistry (total nitrogen, TN; total organic carbon, TOC) to examine baseline climate, historical forestry, and exotic fish introductions. Prior to industrial interventions, gradual increases in TN and TOC reflected natural lake infilling. Between AD 65 and 386, increased chironomid density indicated a water-level transgression driven by a warm, humid climate. In the twentieth century, clear-cut logging (1915–1982) inflicted a severe hydrological shock, shifting the chironomid community and expanding filter-feeders from 22.4% to 33.4%. Post-AD 1989, introducing exotic benthivorous fishes triggered a severe multi-trophic regime shift. This biological invasion collapsed chironomid density and caused an unprecedented exponential upsurge in TN and TOC via metabolic excretion and substrate bioturbation. While chironomid communities showed ecological resilience to macro-climatic transitions, historical logging combined with modern biological invasions has locked this alpine system into a permanently altered, non-recoverable state, shifting modern conservation and restoration baselines.

Contributed talk · C2-03

Divergent fates after parallel historical isolation: habitat area predicts demographic resilience in Japan’s national butterfly across replicated continent-island systems

Zong-Yu Shen*, Jen-Pan Huang, Yu Okamura, Yu-Feng Hsu, Tzong-Han Lin, My-Hang Le, Shao-Ji Hu, Li-Wei Wu, Masaya Yago

Presenting author Zong-Yu Shen
The University Museum, The University of Tokyo

6 November · 14:35–14:50 · C2 · B1B Auditorium

Abstract

Island biogeography predicts that island area governs species richness, yet whether this relationship extends to population-level resilience within species remains poorly understood. Indeed, species-level extinction typically follows a longer process of population loss, making population resilience an urgent conservation target. Testing this requires a species spanning continental and island systems. Sasakia charonda, the national butterfly of Japan and an umbrella species of the Satoyama landscape, provides such a system, with four subspecies spanning continental East Asia, Japan, and Taiwan. Using range-wide sampling and the first chromosome-level reference genome, we integrated phylogenomics, demographic inference, and genotype-environment association to delimit five conservation units (CUs). Two island CUs sharing parallel founder-effect signatures show distinct demographic trajectories, with the larger, more latitudinally extensive island (Japan) maintaining more subpopulations and greater effective population size than the smaller one (Taiwan). A similar pattern appears on the continent, where a highland-restricted lineage with few subpopulations is vulnerable. Habitat area may thus shape demographic resilience through the number of subpopulations a landscape can sustain. The most vulnerable CUs also face the highest projected climate mismatch, compounding their risk. These findings suggest island biogeography principles may extend from species richness to within-species demographic resilience, informing actionable conservation strategies.

Symposium talk · S1-01

A zombie parasitism: How a parasitic barnacle takes over its hosts?

Hsiang-Yin Chen, Chih-Cheng Tsai, Xin-Ci Liang, Yun Chen, Hung-Chang Liu, Chien-Hsun Lee, Jean-Yves Toullec, Wen-San Huang, Benny K.K. Chan, Chi-Ying Lee*

Presenting author Chi-Ying Lee
National Changhua University of Education

6 November · 15:25–15:40 · S1 · B1C Auditorium

Abstract

Rhizocephalans are highly modified and specialized parasites of other crustaceans. Adult rhizocephalan consists of an external reproductive sac (the externa), which is still connected to an internal system of rootlets that ramifies throughout the interior of the host (the interna). These parasites exert control over the host in order to complete their life cycle. We studied the mechanism of host control using an association of the shore crab Metopograpsus thukuhar parasitized by Polyascus planus. First, control over host's reproduction is sex-specific. For females, production of vitellogenin, is not affected, while ovarian maturation is arrested. As for males, production of vitellogenin is induced and testicular maturation hardly affected. Expression of androgenic gland hormone in parasitized males is significantly decreased. Second, the immunity of parasitized hosts is suppressed, with a higher number of hemolymph bacteria, a lower number of circulating hemocytes, and no significant immune responses to tissue-invading rootlets. Necrosis of circulating hemocytes is a key component of the parasite-induced immunosuppression. Finally, an ecdysteroid surge is required to drive the molt cycle, ovarian cycle and secretory cycle of the colleteric gland of sexually mature externa, which collectively influence the reproductive output of (number of larvae released from) the externa.

Symposium talk · S1-02

Phenotypic Similarities between Parasitized Mantids and Male-Specific Wing Dimorphism: Juvenile Hormone as a Possible Shared Regulatory Mechanism

Ming-Chung Chiu, Zih-Ting Chang, Chieh-Ting Tsai, Cheng-Yu Shih

Presenting author Ming-Chung Chiu
Department of Entomology, National Taiwan University

6 November · 15:40–15:55 · S1 · B1C Auditorium

Abstract

Horsehair worms manipulate insect hosts to enter water, but their effects begin earlier. In Hierodula mantids, infection causes gonadal reduction and alters male wing shape and grooved basiconic antennal sensilla. Artificial infections show that these sex-dependent effects emerge during host sexual differentiation, suggesting interference with male differentiation rather than generalized developmental arrest or juvenilization. The uninfected mantid Amantis nawai provides a natural parallel. Taiwanese males are macropterous or micropterous, whereas females are exclusively micropterous. Macropterous males have more antennal sensilla than micropterous males, whose counts are intermediate between macropterous males and females. This pattern suggests partially shared, sex-dependent regulation. Juvenile hormone (JH) may link these traits. In A. nawai, wing-bud differences appear by the third instar, whereas differences in antennal sensilla appear only in adults. JH declines during the final instar, but micropterous males show a transient mid-instar increase. DZNep treatment during this window preliminarily increased wing length and sensilla number and induced wing-lifting. Although causality and DZNep specificity remain unconfirmed, these results support a two-stage model: early establishment of wing morphs followed by JH-sensitive terminal differentiation of wings and antennae. Natural variation may modify this system in A. nawai, while horsehair worms may disrupt the same developmental module in Hierodula.

Symposium talk · S1-03

Natural history and comparative phylogeography of horsehair worms, parasites cycling between aquatic and terrestrial hosts

Shotaro Tani*, Koichiro Kawai, Takuya Sato

Presenting author Shotaro Tani
Center for Ecological Research, Kyoto University

6 November · 15:55–16:10 · S1 · B1C Auditorium

Abstract

Hairworms (Nematomorpha) have a two-phase life cycle: larvae infect aquatic benthic invertebrates, which are then eaten by terrestrial insects in which the worms mature. Mature worms drive their hosts into water, a textbook case of the extended phenotype. However, fundamental information on host range and distribution remains scarce in Asia, including Japan.

Focusing on three Japanese species of the genus Chordodes, we compiled host lists from field surveys and conducted infection experiments on definitive hosts. The results indicated that host specificity is set not by the likelihood of encounter but by a physiological filter: whether larvae can establish and develop once ingested.

Furthermore, molecular phylogeographic analysis uncovered distinct patterns of geographic genetic structure for each species. Given that host dispersal drives gene flow in these parasites—which have limited mobility themselves—we had anticipated high levels of gene flow resulting from the combined dispersal capabilities of both aquatic and terrestrial hosts; however, the data actually revealed genetic divergence associated with specific mountain ranges.

In this talk I use host specificity as a lens for reading these patterns, and ask which of the two environments has shaped the distribution of these parasites.

Symposium talk · S1-04

Sex-specific reproductive strategies in the rhizocephalan

Asami Kajimoto*, Kenji Toyota

Presenting author Asami Kajimoto
Hiroshima University

6 November · 16:10–16:25 · S1 · B1C Auditorium

Abstract

Rhizocephalans (Thecostraca: Cirripedia) are parasitic barnacles that infect diverse decapod crustaceans, including hermit crabs, crabs, and shrimps. Peltogasterella gracilis, a parasite of pagurid hermit crabs, exhibits pronounced sexual dimorphism and sex-specific settlement. Female larvae settle on hermit crab hosts, whereas male larvae settle on immature females and subsequently develop into dwarf males. Here, we investigated how these sex-specific settlement patterns are associated with seasonal sex-ratio variation, sex-biased gene expression, and molecular responses during settlement.

Field surveys revealed seasonal variation in offspring sex ratio: male larvae were absent during summer but were produced from autumn to the following spring. This seasonal pattern was suggested to correspond to the availability of unparasitized hosts for female larvae and immature female parasite bodies for male larvae. Comparative transcriptomic analyses of male and female cyprids identified sex-biased genes potentially involved in sex-specific morphology and settlement. Additionally, comparison of gene expression responses to sex-specific settlement substrates revealed distinct molecular responses between male and female cyprids.

These results suggest that seasonal sex allocation and sex-specific molecular mechanisms of settlement are closely linked in P. gracilis, providing a basis for divergent reproductive strategies between male and female larvae.

Symposium talk · S1-05

A unique mechanism of male-killing induced by symbiotic viruses

Takumi TAKAMATSU*, Maki N. INOUE

Presenting author Takumi Takamatsu
Center for Ecological Research, Kyoto University

6 November · 16:25–16:40 · S1 · B1C Auditorium

Abstract

Maternally inherited female-biased sex ratios are found in many arthropods, and one cause is male-killing, in which males die selectively during embryonic or larval development, resulting in all-female broods. In insects, male-killing is mostly caused by bacteria such as Wolbachia, but some symbiotic viruses also induce it.

Homona magnanima (Lepidoptera: Tortricidae) is a major pest of tea plants in East Asia. Segmented double-stranded RNA viruses, Osugoroshi viruses 1–3 (OGV1–3; Partitiviridae), have been identified as the causative agents of male-killing in this species.
